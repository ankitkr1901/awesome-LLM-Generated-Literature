Knowledge Cutoffs, Temporal Knowledge Boundaries, and Citation Reliability in Large Language Models: A Literature Review 1. Introduction Large language models (LLMs) have substantially changed how information is generated, retrieved, and communicated. However, their capacity to produce fluent and authoritative responses does not guarantee that their knowledge is complete, current, or verifiable. One important limitation is the knowledge cutoff: the temporal boundary associated with the information available during a model's training or subsequent knowledge updates. Information emerging after this boundary may be unavailable to the model unless it has access to external retrieval mechanisms or updated knowledge sources.  Knowledge cutoffs are closely related to two broader problems: temporal knowledge boundaries and citation reliability. Temporal knowledge boundaries concern what a model knows about facts, events, and relationships at particular points in time. Citation reliability concerns whether references generated or supplied by a model actually exist, accurately represent their sources, and substantiate the claims they accompany.  These issues are interconnected. An outdated model may produce historically accurate but presently incorrect information, while a model lacking reliable source attribution may present unsupported claims as established knowledge. This review examines research published on or before 31 December 2024, focusing on conceptual foundations, empirical approaches, principal findings, limitations, and outstanding research questions. 2. Knowledge Cutoffs and Temporal Knowledge Boundaries A knowledge cutoff is not necessarily a precise date after which a model knows nothing. Rather, it represents an approximate boundary of the information incorporated into its training data. The actual distribution of knowledge may be uneven because training corpora contain documents published at different times, duplicated information, historical records, and potentially outdated facts.  Temporal knowledge boundaries therefore involve more than distinguishing pre-cutoff from post-cutoff information. They also concern whether models can distinguish historical facts from current facts, recognize changing relationships, and correctly interpret temporal references.  An important distinction is between static parametric knowledge and dynamic externally accessible knowledge. Parametric knowledge is encoded in model parameters during training, whereas retrieval-augmented systems obtain information from external documents or databases. The latter can improve freshness, although retrieval alone does not guarantee accurate interpretation or attribution.  Research on temporal knowledge editing further demonstrates that incorporating new information can interfere with previously learned information. Yin et al. (2024), in History Matters: Temporal Knowledge Editing in Large Language Model, introduce the AToKe benchmark to investigate whether edited models preserve historical knowledge while learning updated facts. Their findings indicate that conventional editing methods can produce catastrophic forgetting, demonstrating that knowledge updating must account for temporal consistency rather than simply replacing old information. 3. Temporal Reasoning and Knowledge Freshness: Research Themes and Methods A second research theme investigates whether LLMs can reason about time-dependent information rather than merely retrieve isolated facts. Tan, Ng, and Bing (2023) address this problem through TempReason, a benchmark designed to evaluate temporal reasoning at different levels of complexity. Their experiments consider closed-book question answering, open-book question answering, and reasoning-based tasks. The study also explores temporal-span extraction and time-sensitive learning as methods for improving temporal reasoning. This approach is significant because it distinguishes factual recall from the ability to interpret when a fact was true.  ACL Anthology   Complementing this work, Vu et al. (2024) introduce FreshQA, a dynamic question-answering benchmark designed to test knowledge freshness. Their evaluation incorporates more than 50,000 human judgments and examines both factual correctness and hallucination. The authors find that models struggle with rapidly changing information and questions containing false premises. Their proposed FreshPrompt method uses search-engine information to improve responses. These findings suggest that retrieval augmentation can mitigate knowledge staleness, although it cannot automatically eliminate errors in reasoning or interpretation.  ACL Anthology   The difference between these studies is important: TempReason emphasizes temporal reasoning competence, whereas FreshQA emphasizes the freshness and reliability of answers in changing information environments. Together, they demonstrate that temporal knowledge requires both appropriate time-sensitive reasoning and access to sufficiently current evidence. 4. Citation Reliability, Hallucination, and Source Attribution Citation reliability represents a distinct but closely connected dimension of LLM factuality. A generated citation may be unreliable in several ways: the referenced publication may not exist, its bibliographic details may be incorrect, or the cited work may fail to support the associated claim. Consequently, the existence of a reference should not be confused with the evidential validity of its use.  Lin, Hilton, and Evans (2022), through TruthfulQA, establish an important foundation for evaluating factual reliability. Their benchmark contains 817 questions across 38 categories and demonstrates that language models can reproduce popular misconceptions rather than consistently provide truthful answers. Although the study does not specifically investigate citation generation, it illustrates why linguistic confidence and factual accuracy must be evaluated separately.  ACL Anthology   More directly, Walters and Wilder (2023) investigate fabricated bibliographic references produced by ChatGPT-3.5 and GPT-4. Examining 636 references across 84 generated literature reviews, they report fabrication rates of 55% for GPT-3.5 and 18% for GPT-4. They also identify substantive errors among references that correspond to real publications. The improvement in GPT-4 demonstrates progress across model generations, but the remaining errors establish that bibliographic plausibility cannot be treated as bibliographic accuracy.  Scientific Reports+1   Agrawal et al. (2024) extend this discussion in Do Language Models Know When They’re Hallucinating References? Their experiments examine whether models can recognize inconsistencies in generated author lists. They find that language models often produce inconsistent authors for hallucinated references while frequently recalling the authors of genuine publications. This suggests that some internal signals of citation uncertainty may be accessible through consistency-based testing. However, recognizing inconsistencies is not equivalent to independently verifying a publication or establishing that it supports a claim.  ACL Anthology   A complementary approach is presented by Gao et al. (2023) through ALCE (Automatic LLMs' Citation Evaluation). Their benchmark evaluates generated answers along three dimensions: fluency, factual correctness, and citation quality. By combining retrieval and citation generation, ALCE moves evaluation beyond whether an answer sounds plausible and towards whether its claims are supported by retrieved evidence. Its contribution is particularly relevant to scholarly applications, where traceability and claim-level support are essential.  ACL Anthology   Mugaanyi et al. (2024) further examine citation and reference reliability in scholarly writing through a cross-disciplinary evaluation. Their work assesses citation accuracy and DOI correctness, highlighting the importance of evaluating references as structured bibliographic records rather than merely textual elements.  JMIR  5. Comparative Analysis and Methodological Limitations The literature reveals three complementary approaches to improving reliability: 1. Temporal evaluation: Benchmarks such as TempReason and FreshQA assess whether models recognize temporal relationships and respond appropriately to changing information. 2. Knowledge updating and retrieval: Temporal knowledge editing and search-engine augmentation attempt to overcome limitations in static model knowledge. 3. Citation verification: ALCE, bibliographic audits, and consistency-based methods assess whether generated claims and references are supported by evidence. These approaches address different aspects of the same underlying problem. A model may correctly identify an outdated fact but still produce an incorrect citation. Similarly, a retrieval-augmented model may access recent publications but misinterpret their findings or attach citations to unsupported statements.  The broader methodological literature reinforces these concerns. Ji et al. (2023), in their survey of hallucination in natural language generation, distinguish intrinsic hallucinations, which conflict with source material, from extrinsic hallucinations, which introduce unsupported information. Wang et al. (2024) further examine factuality evaluation and identify difficulties in reliably assessing open-ended generated responses.  HKUST Research Portal+1   Several limitations remain evident.  First, knowledge cutoffs are difficult to establish precisely. Publicly declared training dates do not necessarily correspond to the actual temporal distribution of information encoded in model parameters.  Second, temporal benchmarks frequently concentrate on particular domains, question formats, or historical periods. Consequently, their results may not generalize to complex real-world situations involving rapidly changing information.  Third, citation evaluation remains fragmented. Citation existence, bibliographic accuracy, source relevance, and claim-level entailment are separate properties, but many evaluations do not measure all four simultaneously.  Fourth, model performance varies across versions, prompting strategies, retrieval systems, and subject areas. This makes direct comparisons between studies challenging. Human evaluation also introduces potential differences in annotator expertise and interpretation.  Future research should therefore prioritize standardized temporal benchmarks, reproducible knowledge-cutoff assessments, and automated verification combining bibliographic databases with claim-level evidence evaluation. Another important direction is developing models that explicitly communicate uncertainty when information may be outdated or unverifiable. 6. Conclusion and Synthesis The literature establishes that knowledge cutoffs, temporal knowledge boundaries, and citation reliability are interconnected dimensions of LLM trustworthiness. Static parametric knowledge creates challenges for maintaining current information, while temporal reasoning limitations can cause confusion between historical and contemporary facts. Citation hallucinations introduce an additional problem by making unsupported information appear academically credible.  Research from 2022–2024 demonstrates progress through temporal benchmarks, retrieval augmentation, knowledge editing, and citation evaluation. Nevertheless, no single approach adequately addresses all these challenges. Reliable academic use of LLMs requires not simply generating plausible answers, but also recognizing temporal limitations, retrieving authoritative evidence, verifying bibliographic details, and establishing that cited sources genuinely support individual claims.  The central implication is that knowledge freshness, factual correctness, and citation validity must be evaluated as distinct but mutually reinforcing dimensions of LLM reliability.  7.
