Knowledge Cutoffs, Temporal Knowledge Boundaries, and Citation Reliability in Large Language Models: A Literature Review Publication-date restriction: This review considers scholarly works published on or before 31 December 2024. Publications after this date are excluded. 1. Introduction Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed information retrieval, knowledge synthesis, and academic writing. However, their reliability depends partly on the temporal boundaries of the information acquired during training and their ability to distinguish established knowledge from subsequent developments. A central limitation is the knowledge cutoff: the approximate point beyond which information is not reliably represented in a model's training data. Although an LLM may generate convincing responses about recent events, its linguistic competence does not necessarily indicate access to current or accurate information.  Knowledge cutoffs raise two interconnected concerns: temporal knowledge reliability and citation reliability. The former concerns whether information remains accurate at the time of a query, while the latter concerns whether cited sources genuinely exist and substantiate the associated claims. Research published before 2025 increasingly examines these issues through temporal benchmarks, retrieval-augmented generation, factuality evaluation, and empirical citation audits. 2. Conceptual Foundations: Knowledge Cutoffs and Temporal Boundaries A knowledge cutoff represents a limitation associated with the temporal coverage of a model's training data. Brown et al. (2020), in their study of GPT-3, demonstrated how large-scale pretraining enables models to acquire substantial factual and linguistic knowledge from extensive textual corpora. Nevertheless, this knowledge is predominantly parametric: information is encoded in model parameters rather than maintained as a continuously updated database. Consequently, information about political developments, scientific discoveries, institutional changes, and other evolving subjects can become outdated.  Three related concepts help clarify this limitation: - Temporal knowledge boundary: The approximate temporal limit of information reliably represented in a model. - Knowledge freshness: The degree to which an answer reflects information valid at the time of the query. - Temporal validity: Whether a factual statement remains correct within a specified historical or present-time context. These concepts are related but not interchangeable. A model may accurately recall historical information while failing to identify a subsequent change. Furthermore, a training cutoff does not establish that every fact published before that date is represented accurately. It is therefore more appropriate to understand the cutoff as a limitation on information availability and reliability rather than a precise boundary separating known from unknown facts.  Lin, Hilton, and Evans (2022), through the TruthfulQA benchmark, demonstrated that language models can reproduce widely circulated misconceptions. Their findings suggest that the problem extends beyond missing recent information: even knowledge acquired during training may be inaccurate or misleading.  ACL Anthology+1  3. Research Themes and Methodological Developments 3.1 Temporal knowledge and outdated information An important research direction investigates how effectively LLMs handle facts that change over time. Mousavi, Alghisi, and Riccardi (2024) address this problem in DyKnow: Dynamically Verifying Time-Sensitive Factual Knowledge in LLMs. Their approach evaluates model knowledge dynamically against Wikidata rather than relying exclusively on static benchmarks.  Their evaluation covers 24 private and open-source LLMs and examines the effectiveness of knowledge-editing techniques. The researchers report substantial outdatedness, inconsistent responses to slightly modified prompts, and limited improvements from existing editing methods. This is particularly significant because it demonstrates that outdated knowledge is not simply a consequence of an identifiable training cutoff; models may also struggle to maintain consistent representations of facts that have changed.  ACL Anthology  3.2 Retrieval-augmented generation as a response to temporal limitations Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) offers an alternative to relying exclusively on parametric knowledge. Lewis et al. (2020) introduced a framework combining pretrained language models with external, non-parametric memory accessed through information retrieval.  Their research demonstrated improvements across knowledge-intensive NLP tasks, including open-domain question answering. Importantly, retrieved documents can provide information that is absent from the model's original training data. RAG therefore offers a mechanism for improving information freshness, traceability, and factual grounding.  However, retrieval does not automatically guarantee reliability. Its effectiveness depends on the quality, relevance, publication dates, and authority of retrieved documents. An outdated or misleading source may still produce an outdated answer. Moreover, retrieval and generation must be evaluated separately to determine whether the model accurately represents the retrieved evidence.  AI Meta  3.3 Factuality evaluation and hallucination detection A further research theme concerns the assessment of factual accuracy. Min et al. (2023), in their FActScore framework, proposed evaluating long-form generated text by decomposing it into atomic factual statements and measuring the proportion supported by reliable sources.  This method is particularly relevant to temporal knowledge because a response may contain several accurate historical statements alongside one outdated or incorrect claim. Sentence-level or document-level assessments can conceal these differences, whereas atomic evaluation provides greater diagnostic precision.  Manakul, Liusie, and Gales (2023) introduced SelfCheckGPT, a sampling-based hallucination detection method. It examines consistency across multiple independently generated responses, assuming that unreliable information may produce greater factual divergence. Their experiments demonstrated its usefulness in identifying non-factual statements without requiring an external knowledge database.  Although these methods contribute to factuality assessment, they have different limitations. FActScore requires access to reliable supporting evidence, while SelfCheckGPT evaluates consistency rather than establishing truth. A model can consistently reproduce the same outdated information across multiple generations. A generated reference may contain incorrect authors, publication titles, dates, journal information, or DOIs. Even when a reference exists, it may not support the claim for which it is cited.  Gao et al. (2023), through the ALCE benchmark (Automatic LLMs' Citation Evaluation), developed an evaluation framework examining answer fluency, correctness, and citation quality. Their work established the importance of evaluating citations as evidence rather than treating their presence as an indication of factual reliability.  Similarly, Liu, Zhang, and Liang (2023) examined the verifiability of generative search engines through human evaluation. Their findings indicated that only 51.5% of generated sentences were fully supported by citations, while 74.5% of citations supported their associated sentences. These results illustrate the difference between producing citations and providing verifiable evidence.  ACL Anthology+1   Agrawal et al. (2024) investigated whether language models can recognise hallucinated references through consistency checks. Their findings suggest that models sometimes provide inconsistent author information for fabricated references, indicating that internal consistency can help identify suspicious citations. However, such checks cannot independently establish bibliographic authenticity.  Mugaanyi et al. (2024) provide further empirical evidence through a cross-disciplinary evaluation of ChatGPT-generated scholarly references. Their study examined 102 citations across natural sciences and humanities. Although many references corresponded to real publications, DOI accuracy was substantially lower, particularly in the humanities. This demonstrates that bibliographic reliability varies across disciplines and metadata conventions.  ACL Anthology+1  4. Comparative Findings, Limitations, and Open Questions The reviewed literature identifies complementary but distinct dimensions of LLM reliability. Temporal evaluation research, particularly DyKnow, demonstrates the persistence of outdated factual knowledge. RAG research offers a mechanism for accessing newer information, while FActScore and SelfCheckGPT provide methods for evaluating generated factual claims. ALCE and subsequent citation audits extend evaluation to the evidential relationship between statements and references.  Nevertheless, several limitations remain.  First, many benchmarks use static datasets and cannot fully represent continuously changing information. Second, knowledge freshness is frequently evaluated without adequately distinguishing publication date, event date, and the period during which a fact remains valid. Third, retrieval-based systems depend on external sources whose quality and temporal relevance may vary.  Citation evaluation also remains challenging. A bibliographically correct reference does not necessarily provide evidence for the associated claim. Conversely, a relevant source may be cited incorrectly because of fabricated metadata. Existing approaches therefore need to distinguish reference existence, metadata accuracy, source relevance, and claim-level evidential support.  Important open questions include how models should communicate uncertainty about temporal boundaries, how frequently external knowledge should be refreshed, and how automated systems can verify both bibliographic metadata and the temporal validity of cited evidence. 5. Conclusion Research available by December 2024 establishes that knowledge cutoffs, temporal validity, factuality, and citation reliability are interconnected challenges in LLM development. Parametric knowledge provides substantial linguistic and factual capabilities but cannot guarantee current information. Retrieval-augmented approaches improve access to external knowledge, while factuality benchmarks and citation-verification methods provide complementary safeguards.  Ultimately, trustworthy LLM-generated knowledge requires more than fluent responses or plausible references. It requires temporally appropriate information, independently verifiable sources, accurate bibliographic metadata, and demonstrable support for individual claims.
