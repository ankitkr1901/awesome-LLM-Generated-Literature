Knowledge Cutoffs, Temporal Knowledge Boundaries, and Citation Reliability in Large Language Models 1. Introduction Large language models (LLMs) have transformed information retrieval, knowledge synthesis, and academic writing. However, their knowledge is not continuously updated. Information acquired during pretraining is constrained by the training corpus, its collection period, and subsequent model updates. This creates a temporal boundary beyond which a model may lack reliable knowledge of events, publications, institutional changes, scientific discoveries, and other developments. Even when an LLM produces a plausible answer about recent events, it may rely on outdated information or generate unsupported claims.  Knowledge cutoffs and temporal knowledge boundaries are therefore closely related to factual accuracy, hallucination, and citation reliability. A knowledge cutoff refers broadly to the temporal limit of information incorporated into a model's training or updating process, whereas a temporal knowledge boundary describes the limits of the model's ability to retrieve, interpret, and distinguish facts across different periods. These concepts are not identical: a model may possess historical information beyond its effective reasoning capacity, while retrieval-augmented systems may access current information that was never incorporated into model parameters.  This review examines research published on or before 31 December 2024, focusing on temporal knowledge evaluation, knowledge updating, hallucination detection, and citation verification. 2. Knowledge Cutoffs and Temporal Knowledge Boundaries An LLM's knowledge is distributed across its learned parameters rather than stored as a conventional, explicitly timestamped database. Consequently, knowing when a model was trained does not establish precisely which facts it knows or whether it can distinguish historical from current information.  Jang et al. (2022), in their introduction of TemporalWiki, describe this problem as temporal misalignment: language models become outdated as the information represented in their training data changes. Their benchmark uses successive Wikipedia and Wikidata snapshots to evaluate knowledge acquisition and retention over time. This approach demonstrates that model knowledge should be understood as evolving rather than static.  Temporal knowledge boundaries also involve distinctions between historical and present-oriented questions. For example, asking who held a political office in a particular year requires historical retrieval, whereas asking who currently holds that office requires up-to-date information. A model may answer the first question correctly but fail on the second because its internal representation reflects an earlier state of the world.  An important distinction is therefore necessary between a model's training-data cutoff, its effective knowledge boundary, and the temporal validity of an individual claim. These boundaries may differ substantially. 3. Research Themes and Methodological Approaches 3.1 Benchmarking temporal knowledge and outdated information A major research direction involves developing benchmarks that evaluate whether language models can distinguish current facts from outdated information.  Vu et al. (2024), in FreshLLMs: Refreshing Large Language Models with Search Engine Augmentation, introduce FreshQA, a dynamic question-answering benchmark designed to test knowledge of rapidly changing information and the ability to challenge false premises. Their evaluation incorporates more than 50,000 human judgments. The authors find that both open-source and closed-source models struggle with fast-changing facts and questions requiring the correction of outdated or incorrect assumptions. Their FreshPrompt approach improves performance by organizing search-engine information within prompts.  Meem et al. (2024) extend this research through PAT-Questions, a benchmark for present-anchored temporal question answering. Unlike conventional temporal benchmarks, which specify historical dates, PAT-Questions evaluates expressions such as "currently", "previous", and "last". It includes single-hop and multi-hop questions and supports automatic answer refreshing through knowledge-graph queries. Their experiments demonstrate that retrieval-augmented generation does not completely eliminate temporal reasoning difficulties.  These studies reveal an important methodological development: evaluating temporal knowledge requires dynamic benchmarks rather than static datasets whose answers remain unchanged despite developments in the real world. 3.2 Knowledge updating and temporal knowledge editing Another research theme examines how pretrained models can incorporate new facts without losing previously acquired information.  Jang et al. (2022) demonstrate that continual learning using changes between successive Wikipedia snapshots can support knowledge updating with substantially less computational expense than retraining on complete snapshots. Their TemporalWiki benchmark also examines whether updated models retain historical information.  Yin et al. (2024), in History Matters: Temporal Knowledge Editing in Large Language Model, introduce AToKe, a benchmark for temporal knowledge editing. Their findings reveal that conventional model-editing approaches can successfully incorporate new information but may catastrophically forget historical facts. Their proposed Multi-Editing with Time Objective (METO) framework attempts to preserve both historical and updated knowledge by explicitly incorporating temporal objectives.  Similarly, research by Chen et al. (2024) investigates temporal knowledge question answering through Abstract Reasoning Induction. Their framework separates knowledge-independent reasoning from knowledge-based reasoning, demonstrating the potential benefits of combining temporal reasoning with factual knowledge support.  Collectively, these studies indicate that updating an LLM is not simply a matter of replacing an outdated fact. Reliable updating requires maintaining temporal distinctions, historical consistency, and the ability to reason about when particular facts were valid. 3.3 Retrieval augmentation and factual reliability Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) represents an important response to the limitations of parametric knowledge. Lewis et al. (2020) introduce a framework combining pretrained language models with external, non-parametric knowledge retrieved from a document index. Their experiments demonstrate improvements in factual specificity and diversity compared with parametric-only generation.  RAG provides a mechanism for accessing information beyond the original training corpus. However, retrieval does not automatically guarantee correctness. Retrieved documents may be outdated, irrelevant, contradictory, or insufficient to support a generated statement. The model must also interpret publication dates, distinguish historical from current claims, and correctly associate evidence with its corresponding assertions.  FreshLLMs and PAT-Questions reinforce this limitation: even when external information is available, temporal reasoning and accurate integration of retrieved evidence remain challenging. 3.4 Hallucination and citation reliability Citation reliability is a distinct but closely connected problem. An LLM may generate a factually plausible statement while attaching an incorrect, irrelevant, or nonexistent reference. Consequently, factual correctness and citation correctness should be evaluated separately.  Lin et al. (2022), through the TruthfulQA benchmark, investigate whether models reproduce falsehoods present in human-generated text. Their experiments show that model scale alone does not guarantee truthfulness. This finding is relevant to temporal knowledge because outdated or misleading information can remain linguistically plausible even when circumstances have changed.  Manakul et al. (2023) introduce SelfCheckGPT, a black-box hallucination detection method based on comparing multiple sampled responses. The underlying assumption is that inconsistent answers may indicate unreliable factual knowledge. Their experiments demonstrate the potential of sampling-based methods for identifying non-factual statements without requiring external databases or access to model probabilities. However, consistency is not equivalent to truth: a model can repeatedly reproduce the same incorrect information.  More directly, Gao et al. (2023) introduce ALCE (Automatic LLMs' Citation Evaluation), a benchmark for evaluating citation-supported generation. Their framework assesses fluency, answer correctness, and citation quality. Their experiments reveal substantial shortcomings in citation completeness, with even the strongest evaluated systems failing to provide complete citation support approximately half the time on the ELI5 dataset.  Li et al. (2023), through HaluEval, provide another important contribution by developing a large-scale benchmark for hallucination evaluation. Their findings indicate that external knowledge and reasoning processes can improve hallucination recognition, although reliable identification remains challenging.  Together, these studies establish that citation reliability involves more than producing references in an academically appropriate format. A reliable citation must correspond to a real publication, contain accurate bibliographic information, support the associated claim, and be temporally appropriate. 4. Limitations and Open Research Questions Despite considerable progress, several limitations remain.  First, static evaluation is insufficient. Traditional benchmarks cannot adequately measure knowledge changes because their answers may become outdated. Dynamic benchmarks require continuous updating and transparent documentation of evaluation dates.  Second, temporal reasoning remains underdeveloped. Models may retrieve relevant information but fail to distinguish when a fact became valid, when it ceased to be valid, or whether two sources describe different historical periods.  Third, retrieval does not guarantee citation reliability. A system may retrieve authoritative publications but misrepresent their findings, assign citations to unsupported claims, or confuse publication dates with online indexing dates.  Fourth, hallucination detection has methodological limitations. Agreement between generated responses does not establish factual correctness, while automated citation metrics may overlook subtle differences between a source's actual findings and a model's interpretation.  Finally, research requires more comprehensive evaluation across disciplines, languages, and publication types. Particularly important questions concern whether models can recognize their own knowledge boundaries, communicate uncertainty appropriately, verify publication metadata, and distinguish genuine scholarly evidence from fabricated references.  Future research should investigate integrated evaluation frameworks combining temporal validity, factual accuracy, source provenance, bibliographic verification, and claim-level citation entailment. 5. Conclusion The literature demonstrates that knowledge cutoffs are not merely technical restrictions on training data; they create broader challenges involving temporal reasoning, factual updating, and scholarly citation reliability. TemporalWiki, FreshQA, and PAT-Questions establish increasingly sophisticated approaches to evaluating outdated knowledge. Research on knowledge editing highlights the importance of preserving historical facts while incorporating new information. Meanwhile, RAG and citation-evaluation frameworks demonstrate the potential and limitations of external evidence integration.  Overall, reliable LLM knowledge systems require more than larger training datasets or external retrieval. They require explicit temporal awareness, verifiable sources, claim-level evidence assessment, and transparent communication of uncertainty. These capabilities are essential if LLMs are to become dependable tools for academic research and knowledge production.
