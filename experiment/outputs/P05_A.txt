Knowledge Cutoffs, Temporal Knowledge Boundaries, and Citation Reliability in Large Language Models: A Literature Review 1. Introduction Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed information retrieval, academic writing, and knowledge-intensive reasoning. However, their ability to generate fluent and authoritative responses does not necessarily indicate that their information is current, factually accurate, or supported by verifiable sources. Three closely related issues are particularly important: knowledge cutoffs, temporal knowledge boundaries, and citation reliability. A knowledge cutoff generally refers to the latest period represented in a model's training data, whereas a temporal knowledge boundary concerns the limits of the model's ability to retrieve, interpret, and reason about information associated with particular points in time. Citation reliability concerns whether references generated or supplied by an LLM actually exist and accurately support the claims attributed to them.  These issues are interconnected because a model may present outdated information as current, combine facts from different historical periods, or generate plausible but fabricated references. Understanding them is essential for evaluating LLMs in academic and professional applications. 2. Knowledge Cutoffs and Temporal Knowledge Boundaries The conventional understanding of a knowledge cutoff assumes that a model's parametric knowledge reflects information available up to a particular training-data date. However, this date should not be interpreted as a precise boundary separating known from unknown information. Training datasets contain documents published at different times, duplicated material, archived content, and potentially outdated information. Consequently, the effective temporal coverage of a model can vary across subjects and individual facts.  Cheng et al. (2024), in Dated Data: Tracing Knowledge Cutoffs in Large Language Models, investigate this distinction by introducing the concept of an effective cutoff. Their findings demonstrate that the effective temporal boundary can differ from the officially reported cutoff because of temporal inconsistencies in web-crawled datasets and complications arising from data deduplication. This suggests that a single cutoff date is an inadequate representation of a model's actual knowledge distribution.  Li et al. (2025) extend the discussion through a systematic survey of LLM knowledge boundaries. They conceptualise knowledge boundaries in terms of what models can memorise and successfully utilise, highlighting the distinction between possessing information and reliably producing it. Their work establishes a broader framework for understanding why apparently knowledgeable models can generate inaccurate responses.  Temporal knowledge boundaries also involve the ability to distinguish historical facts from contemporary developments. For example, an LLM may correctly describe an organisation's leadership in 2022 but incorrectly present that information as valid in 2026. Thus, temporal reliability requires more than factual recall: it requires identifying when information was valid and whether subsequent developments have superseded it. 3. Research Themes and Evaluation Methods Recent research has developed several complementary approaches to investigating temporal knowledge limitations.  Temporal probing and benchmark construction. One major research direction involves testing models with questions whose answers depend on specific historical dates. Cheng et al. (2024) use different versions of datasets to estimate the effective temporal coverage of models. Similarly, Zhu et al. (2025), in EvolveBench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Assessing Temporal Awareness in LLMs on Evolving Knowledge, evaluate temporal competence across five dimensions: cognition, awareness, trustworthiness, understanding, and reasoning. Their evaluation of 15 LLMs demonstrates that even comparatively capable models struggle when external information conflicts with the temporal context of a question. These approaches are valuable because they examine not merely whether a model knows a fact but whether it understands the fact's temporal validity. (2023) introduce FActScore, which decomposes generated responses into atomic factual statements and calculates the proportion supported by reliable sources. Their findings illustrate why evaluating an entire response as simply correct or incorrect is insufficient. A response can contain several accurate statements alongside unsupported or fabricated details. This method is particularly relevant to literature reviews, where individual claims, publication dates, and author attributions require separate verification.  ACL Anthology   Retrieval-augmented generation. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) offers a potential solution to the limitations of static parametric knowledge. Lewis et al. (2020), in Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, combine pretrained parametric memory with an external non-parametric document index. Their research demonstrates that retrieving relevant documents can improve the specificity and factuality of generated responses. Rather than relying exclusively on information encoded during training, a model can consult an external knowledge repository.  Asai et al. (2024) advance this approach through Self-RAG, which incorporates adaptive retrieval and self-reflection. Instead of retrieving a fixed number of passages for every query, the model learns when retrieval is necessary and evaluates the relevance of retrieved evidence. Their experiments report improvements in factuality and citation accuracy. Nevertheless, retrieval does not automatically guarantee correctness: outdated documents, irrelevant passages, and conflicting sources can still undermine the generated response.  AI Meta+1  4. Citation Reliability and Bibliographic Hallucinations Citation reliability represents a distinct but closely related dimension of LLM factuality. A reference may be entirely fabricated, may identify a genuine publication with incorrect bibliographic details, or may exist but fail to support the claim associated with it. These problems are commonly described as bibliographic hallucinations and citation-grounding failures.  Walters and Wilder (2023), in their empirical study Fabrication and Errors in the Bibliographic Citations Generated by ChatGPT, investigate 636 references produced in 84 AI-generated literature reviews. They report that 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated. Furthermore, 43% of genuine GPT-3.5 references and 24% of genuine GPT-4 references contained substantive citation errors. Although the newer model performed considerably better, the findings demonstrate that improved language-generation capabilities do not necessarily ensure bibliographic accuracy.  Scientific Reports   Gao et al. (2023) address this issue through ALCE (Automatic LLMs' Citation Evaluation), a benchmark designed to assess generated citations in terms of fluency, correctness, and citation quality. Their experiments reveal that even strong systems frequently fail to provide complete evidential support. On the ELI5 dataset, the best-performing systems lacked complete citation support in approximately half of their responses. This distinction is important: a genuine reference is not necessarily a relevant or sufficient reference. Citation reliability must therefore be evaluated at both bibliographic and evidential levels.  ACL Anthology   Huang et al. (2025), in their survey of hallucination research, further situate citation errors within the broader problem of generating plausible but non-factual content. Taken together, these studies suggest that citation verification requires checking publication existence, author identity, bibliographic metadata, source relevance, and the actual evidential relationship between a source and a claim. First, knowledge cutoffs are difficult to measure precisely. A model's ability to answer questions about recent events does not necessarily establish when the relevant information entered its training corpus. Effective knowledge boundaries may differ across domains, languages, and document collections.  Second, existing temporal benchmarks frequently rely on predefined questions and relatively structured datasets. They may not adequately represent real-world situations involving ambiguous dates, evolving institutional information, conflicting historical accounts, or rapidly changing scientific knowledge.  Third, citation evaluation remains challenging. Automated systems can establish whether a publication exists, but determining whether it genuinely supports a particular interpretation often requires domain expertise. Moreover, a correct citation can still be misleading when its findings are taken out of context.  Fourth, retrieval-based approaches introduce additional dependencies, including search quality, database coverage, source credibility, and document freshness. Consequently, external retrieval reduces dependence on outdated parametric knowledge but does not eliminate factual or citation errors.  An emerging research direction is the development of more comprehensive temporal reasoning frameworks. Li et al. (2026), in Static Models, Dynamic World: A Unified Perspective on Temporal Perception in Large Language Models, examine temporal grounding, stale knowledge, and the distinction between information available internally and information requiring external evidence. This perspective highlights the importance of distinguishing historical knowledge, current factual knowledge, and genuinely uncertain future information.  ACL Anthology   Future research should therefore investigate: - Reliable methods for estimating topic-specific and source-specific knowledge cutoffs. - Benchmarks that measure temporal consistency across different historical and contemporary contexts. - Automated verification of references, DOIs, publication metadata, and claim-level evidential support. - Methods for detecting outdated or temporally conflicting retrieved documents. - Better mechanisms for uncertainty communication, abstention, and disclosure of information limitations. - Standardised evaluation frameworks combining factual accuracy, temporal validity, provenance, and citation correctness. 6. Conclusion and Synthesis The literature demonstrates that knowledge cutoffs, temporal knowledge boundaries, and citation reliability are interconnected dimensions of LLM trustworthiness. A declared training cutoff does not accurately describe every piece of information a model has acquired, while factual fluency does not establish temporal correctness. Similarly, generating a plausible reference does not demonstrate that the cited publication exists or supports the associated claim.  Research on temporal probing, factuality benchmarks, retrieval-augmented generation, and citation evaluation offers promising methods for addressing these challenges. However, no single approach completely resolves them. Reliable academic applications of LLMs require a combination of temporal awareness, evidence-based retrieval, source verification, and human oversight. Ultimately, the objective should not simply be to develop models that produce convincing answers, but models that can distinguish what they know, when that knowledge is valid, and what evidence supports their conclusions.
