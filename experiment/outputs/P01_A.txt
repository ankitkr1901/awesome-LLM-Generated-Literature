Knowledge Cutoffs, Temporal Knowledge Boundaries, and Citation Reliability in Large Language Models: A Literature Review 1. Introduction Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed information retrieval, academic writing, and knowledge-based question answering. However, their reliability depends partly on the temporal scope and accuracy of the information acquired during training. Unlike continuously updated databases, most LLMs primarily encode knowledge through training on relatively static datasets. Consequently, their responses may reflect outdated information, omit recent developments, or present uncertain facts as established knowledge. These limitations are particularly significant in academic research, where factual accuracy, source traceability, and citation integrity are essential.  Three related concepts are central to understanding this problem: knowledge cutoffs, which indicate the approximate temporal boundary of a model's training knowledge; temporal knowledge boundaries, which concern its ability to distinguish historical, current, and future information; and citation reliability, which concerns whether references generated by an LLM exist, contain accurate bibliographic details, and genuinely support the associated claims. This review examines research addressing these concepts, their methodological approaches, major findings, and unresolved challenges. 2. Knowledge Cutoffs and Temporal Knowledge Boundaries A knowledge cutoff represents the approximate endpoint of information incorporated into a model's training process. It does not necessarily establish an exact date after which the model possesses no knowledge. Training datasets may contain documents from different periods, and subsequent fine-tuning or information updates can modify the model's knowledge. Furthermore, a model may correctly infer recent developments from contextual information without having encountered them during training.  Lazaridou et al. (2021), in Mind the Gap: Assessing Temporal Generalization in Neural Language Models, investigate the difficulty of applying language models to continuously evolving information. Using temporally separated language-modelling datasets, they demonstrate that model performance deteriorates when predicting future linguistic patterns beyond the training period. Importantly, increasing model size alone does not adequately resolve temporal degradation. Their findings establish that temporal generalization is a distinct challenge rather than simply a consequence of insufficient model capacity.  Dhingra et al. (2021), in Adapting Language Models to Temporal Knowledge, extend this discussion to factual knowledge. They introduce a diagnostic dataset for examining changing facts and demonstrate that models trained exclusively on recent information can perform better on current and future questions but worse on historical questions. Their experiments also show that incorporating timestamps into training improves the representation of historical facts and uncertainty about future events. This suggests that temporal awareness requires more than frequently refreshing training datasets: models must also represent when information was valid.  proceedings.neurips.cc+1  3. Research on Temporal Reasoning and Evaluation Subsequent research has increasingly examined whether LLMs can interpret temporal expressions, distinguish historical from contemporary facts, and reason about changing circumstances.  Tan, Ng, and Bing (2023) address these challenges through Towards Benchmarking and Improving the Temporal Reasoning Capability of Large Language Models. They introduce TempReason, a benchmark containing questions across three levels of temporal reasoning. Their methodology combines closed-book question answering, open-book question answering, and reasoning-based evaluation. They additionally investigate temporal span extraction and time-sensitive reinforcement learning as approaches to improving temporal understanding. Their work highlights the importance of evaluating not only factual recall but also the ability to establish relationships between events and their respective time periods.  Similarly, Jain et al. (2023), in Do Language Models Have a Common Sense regarding Time? Revisiting Temporal Commonsense Reasoning in the Era of Large Language Models, evaluate eight LLMs across six datasets using three prompting strategies. Their investigation considers temporal continuity, event sequencing, and progression. The study provides evidence that general language proficiency does not necessarily translate into consistent temporal reasoning.  More recently, Wallat et al. (2025), in A Study into Investigating Temporal Robustness of LLMs, develop eight tests to examine temporal sensitivity in six LLMs. Their zero-shot experiments reveal weaknesses in handling temporal reformulations and different granularities of time expressions. This indicates that seemingly equivalent questions can produce inconsistent answers when their temporal framing changes.  Zhu et al. (2025) extend this research through EvolveBench, a benchmark assessing five dimensions: cognition, awareness, trustworthiness, understanding, and reasoning. Their evaluation of 15 LLMs demonstrates that even comparatively capable models struggle with temporally misaligned information. Collectively, these studies suggest that temporal reliability involves several interconnected abilities: recognizing temporal context, identifying outdated information, distinguishing valid historical facts from current facts, and refusing questions involving invalid or unknowable temporal assumptions. A generated citation may be entirely fabricated, refer to a genuine publication with incorrect metadata, or identify a real source that does not substantiate the associated claim. These failures are particularly problematic because scholarly references often appear authoritative even when their contents have not been verified.  Walters and Wilder (2023), in Fabrication and Errors in the Bibliographic Citations Generated by ChatGPT, systematically examine citations produced by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. Their study analyses 636 references generated across 84 literature reviews covering 42 multidisciplinary topics. They report that 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated. Additionally, substantive errors occurred in 43% and 24% of the respective models' genuine citations. These findings demonstrate that improved model capabilities can reduce citation fabrication without eliminating bibliographic inaccuracies.  Scientific Reports+1   Mugaanyi et al. (2024), in Evaluation of Large Language Model Performance and Reliability for Citations and References in Scholarly Writing: Cross-Disciplinary Study, investigate citation generation across natural sciences and humanities. Their evaluation examines 102 references generated by GPT-3.5, checking publication existence, bibliographic accuracy, relevance, and DOI reliability. Although approximately three-quarters of the references were found to exist, DOI accuracy varied considerably across disciplines. The authors identify disciplinary differences in DOI practices as an important factor affecting citation reliability.  These studies reveal that citation reliability cannot be measured solely by determining whether a publication exists. Accurate author attribution, publication details, DOI validity, and relevance to the cited argument must also be evaluated.  doi.org+1  5. Methodological Approaches and Potential Solutions The literature employs several complementary methodologies: - Temporal benchmarking: Researchers construct datasets containing time-dependent questions to evaluate historical recall, current factual accuracy, and temporal reasoning. - Controlled prompting experiments: Models receive differently phrased or temporally framed questions to identify inconsistencies in their responses. - Bibliographic verification: Generated references are checked against scholarly databases, publisher records, and DOI registries. - Comparative model evaluation: Different model generations and architectures are assessed to determine whether improved capabilities translate into greater temporal and bibliographic reliability. One promising mitigation strategy is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Lewis et al. (2020), in Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, establish a framework that combines parametric language-model knowledge with externally retrieved documents. This approach can provide access to information beyond the training cutoff and enable source-grounded responses. Nevertheless, retrieval does not automatically guarantee reliability: retrieved documents may themselves be outdated, irrelevant, or incorrectly interpreted.  Huang et al. (2025), in their survey A Survey on Hallucination in Large Language Models: Principles, Taxonomy, Challenges, and Open Questions, provide a broader framework for understanding hallucinations and their mitigation. Their work reinforces the importance of grounding, factual verification, and systematic evaluation.  ACM Transactions on Information Systems  6. Limitations and Open Research Questions Despite considerable progress, several limitations remain. First, temporal benchmarks frequently rely on predefined datasets and selected factual domains, potentially limiting their generalizability to real-world information environments. Second, proprietary models may undergo undocumented training updates, making precise knowledge-cutoff comparisons difficult. Third, many citation studies examine particular model versions and relatively small samples, limiting conclusions about contemporary models and diverse academic disciplines.  An additional concern is the distinction between citation existence and evidential validity. A reference may be genuine but irrelevant, misrepresented, or unable to support the specific claim. Future research should therefore investigate claim-to-source entailment, publication-date sensitivity, citation provenance, and reproducibility across model versions.  Further work is also needed to develop systems that explicitly communicate temporal uncertainty, distinguish information available at different dates, and recognize when external verification is necessary. 7. Conclusion The literature establishes that knowledge cutoffs, temporal reasoning, and citation reliability are closely interconnected challenges affecting LLM trustworthiness. Research demonstrates that static training knowledge becomes increasingly inadequate in dynamic information environments, while temporal benchmarks reveal persistent weaknesses in understanding changing facts. Studies of scholarly references further demonstrate that plausible-looking citations cannot be assumed to be authentic or accurate. Retrieval-augmented generation, temporal metadata, and independent bibliographic verification offer promising solutions, although none independently guarantees reliability. Ultimately, trustworthy LLM-assisted research requires transparent temporal boundaries, evidence-grounded generation, and systematic verification of both factual claims and their supporting references.
