Knowledge Cutoffs, Temporal Knowledge Boundaries, and Citation Reliability in Large Language Models Literature Review 1. Introduction Large Language Models (LLMs) have significantly transformed information retrieval, academic writing, knowledge synthesis, and scholarly communication. However, their increasing use in knowledge-intensive tasks has raised important questions concerning the currency, accuracy, and verifiability of the information they generate. Three closely related issues are particularly significant: knowledge cutoffs, temporal knowledge boundaries, and citation reliability.  A knowledge cutoff generally refers to the approximate endpoint of the information available during a model's training or knowledge acquisition. Information emerging after this point may not be represented in its internal parameters unless the model receives updated information through mechanisms such as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), fine-tuning, or external tools. Temporal knowledge boundaries extend this concept by considering whether a model can distinguish historical facts, current information, evolving circumstances, and events that have not yet occurred. Citation reliability, meanwhile, concerns whether references generated by an LLM correspond to genuine publications and accurately represent their bibliographic details and supporting evidence.  These issues are interconnected because a model that cannot reliably distinguish outdated information from current knowledge may also produce misleading explanations and inaccurate scholarly references. This review examines relevant research, evaluation methods, empirical findings, limitations, and emerging research directions. 2. Knowledge Cutoffs and Temporal Knowledge Boundaries LLMs acquire much of their factual knowledge through statistical patterns learned from large training corpora. Although this process enables impressive factual recall, it does not guarantee that the information remains current. Dhingra et al. (2021), in Adapting Language Models to Temporal Knowledge, investigate how language models represent facts that change over time. Their research introduces a diagnostic dataset for evaluating temporal factual knowledge and demonstrates that training exclusively on recent information can improve performance on contemporary questions while weakening recall of historical facts. The authors further demonstrate the benefits of incorporating temporal information into language modelling, particularly for preserving historical knowledge and reasoning about future uncertainty.  This finding highlights an important distinction: knowledge updating should not simply replace old information with new information. Historical and contemporary facts may both be correct when associated with different periods. For example, an individual may have held different institutional positions at different times. A reliable model must therefore identify not only what is true but also when it was true.  Li et al. (2025), in Knowledge Boundary of Large Language Models: A Survey, provide a broader conceptual framework for understanding these limitations. They classify knowledge boundaries and review approaches for identifying and mitigating them. Their work suggests that knowledge limitations involve more than missing information: they also concern the model's ability to recognize what it knows, what it does not know, and what information requires external verification.  More recent research by Mousavi, Alghisi, and Riccardi (2024), presented in DyKnow: Dynamically Verifying Time-Sensitive Factual Knowledge in LLMs, investigates whether models can identify outdated factual information. The authors evaluate 24 open-source and proprietary LLMs against dynamically updated Wikidata information. Their findings reveal substantial outdatedness, inconsistent responses to slightly modified prompts, and limited effectiveness of existing knowledge-editing techniques.  Together, these studies demonstrate that knowledge cutoffs should not be understood as perfectly defined chronological boundaries. A model may retain information published after an assumed cutoff through subsequent updates, while failing to recall certain facts published considerably earlier. Consequently, temporal knowledge boundaries are better understood as variable limits on factual availability, retrieval, and temporal interpretation. 3. Research Methods for Evaluating Temporal Knowledge Research investigating temporal knowledge limitations generally employs three methodological approaches.  First, benchmark-based evaluation examines model responses to questions involving historical, contemporary, and changing facts. Zhu et al. (2025), in EvolveBench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Assessing Temporal Awareness in LLMs on Evolving Knowledge, introduce a benchmark covering multiple dimensions of temporal competence. These include historical knowledge, awareness of temporal misalignment, and the ability to identify invalid timestamps. This approach extends conventional factual question-answering evaluation by examining whether models understand the temporal context of information.  Second, dynamic knowledge verification compares model-generated answers with continuously updated external knowledge sources. DyKnow illustrates this approach by using Wikidata to evaluate time-sensitive factual claims. Unlike static benchmarks, dynamic evaluation can identify discrepancies between a model's internal knowledge and changing external reality.  Third, knowledge-editing experiments investigate whether outdated information can be corrected without damaging previously acquired knowledge. Yin et al. (2024), in History Matters: Temporal Knowledge Editing in Large Language Model, introduce the AToKe benchmark and propose Multi-Editing with Time Objective (METO). Their findings indicate that conventional editing techniques can introduce catastrophic forgetting of historical facts, whereas their proposed approach improves the retention of historical knowledge while incorporating updated information.  Although these methods provide complementary perspectives, their results are not directly interchangeable. Benchmark performance measures task-specific competence, dynamic verification measures factual currency, and knowledge-editing experiments measure the consequences of modifying internal representations. 4. Citation Reliability and Bibliographic Hallucination Citation reliability represents a distinct but closely connected dimension of LLM trustworthiness. Unlike ordinary factual hallucination, bibliographic hallucination occurs when a model generates references that appear academically credible but contain fabricated titles, incorrect authors, nonexistent publications, inaccurate publication years, or invalid DOIs.  Walters and Wilder (2023), in their study Fabrication and Errors in the Bibliographic Citations Generated by ChatGPT, provide important empirical evidence. They examined 636 references generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 across 84 literature reviews covering 42 multidisciplinary topics. Their findings indicated that 55% of GPT-3.5 references and 18% of GPT-4 references were fabricated. Furthermore, substantive errors occurred in 43% and 24% of the respective non-fabricated references. Although GPT-4 demonstrated substantial improvement, the results established that bibliographic accuracy could not be assumed even when the generated references appeared plausible.  Similarly, Mugaanyi et al. (2024), in Evaluation of Large Language Model Performance and Reliability for Citations and References in Scholarly Writing: Cross-Disciplinary Study, investigated citation generation in natural sciences and humanities. Their evaluation found that 72.7% of natural science citations and 76.6% of humanities citations could be confirmed as existing publications. However, accurate DOI identification and bibliographic details varied considerably across disciplines. The findings suggest that citation reliability depends not only on model capabilities but also on disciplinary conventions and the availability of structured bibliographic information.  These findings are consistent with the broader discussion of hallucination presented by Ji et al. (2023) in Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation. The authors distinguish between different forms of hallucination and examine their causes, evaluation methods, and mitigation strategies. In academic writing, fabricated references can be particularly problematic because linguistic fluency and realistic bibliographic formatting may create an unwarranted impression of scholarly authenticity.  An important distinction must therefore be maintained between factual accuracy and citation accuracy. A model may generate a correct statement while attaching an irrelevant or fabricated reference. Conversely, a genuine publication may be cited inaccurately or interpreted beyond the evidence it provides. Citation verification must consequently examine both the existence of a publication and the relevance of its findings to the associated claim. 5. Mitigation Strategies and Emerging Approaches Research identifies several approaches to reducing temporal inaccuracies and improving citation reliability.  Retrieval-augmented generation is particularly relevant. Lewis et al. (2020), in Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, demonstrate how combining parametric model knowledge with externally retrieved documents can improve factual generation. Their approach provides access to information beyond the model's original training corpus and offers opportunities for evidence-based responses.  Nevertheless, retrieval does not automatically guarantee reliability. The quality of generated answers remains dependent on document relevance, source credibility, publication dates, retrieval accuracy, and the model's ability to represent evidence faithfully.  Knowledge editing offers another potential solution by updating specific factual information within model parameters. However, the findings of Yin et al. (2024) and Mousavi et al. (2024) indicate that existing approaches can struggle to preserve historical information and maintain consistency.  For citation generation, external bibliographic verification provides a more direct intervention. Checking publication titles, author identities, publication venues, and DOIs against authoritative scholarly databases can reduce fabricated and inaccurate references. Such verification should ideally be accompanied by claim-to-source checking to ensure that cited publications actually support the accompanying statements. 6. Limitations and Open Research Questions Despite considerable progress, several limitations remain in the literature.  First, the definition of a knowledge cutoff is often insufficiently precise. Model developers may disclose approximate training-data dates, but these do not necessarily indicate the actual chronological limits of knowledge retained by a model. Different facts may have different levels of representation and accessibility.  Second, many temporal evaluation benchmarks depend on structured knowledge bases such as Wikidata. Although these resources facilitate systematic comparisons, they may contain incomplete, delayed, or disputed information. Consequently, benchmark results do not always represent the complexity of real-world knowledge.  Third, existing research frequently evaluates temporal reasoning and citation reliability separately. More integrated investigations are needed to determine whether outdated parametric knowledge increases the likelihood of fabricated references or inaccurate source attribution.  Fourth, retrieval-based approaches introduce additional challenges involving source credibility, document versioning, conflicting evidence, and citation provenance. A retrieved document may be authentic but outdated, while a recent document may not represent authoritative evidence.  Several important research questions therefore remain: 1. How can LLMs communicate uncertainty about the temporal validity of their knowledge? 2. How can models preserve historical information while incorporating newly established facts? 3. Can automated citation-verification systems reliably evaluate both bibliographic authenticity and evidential relevance? 4. How can temporal benchmarks be standardized across models, languages, disciplines, and changing knowledge environments? 5. What mechanisms can ensure that models distinguish verified information from plausible but unsupported generated content? Future research should prioritize integrated evaluation frameworks combining temporal factuality, source provenance, citation verification, and calibrated uncertainty. 7. Conclusion and Synthesis The literature demonstrates that knowledge cutoffs, temporal knowledge boundaries, and citation reliability are interconnected challenges affecting the trustworthiness of LLMs. Existing research establishes that models can retain outdated information, inconsistently interpret changing facts, and generate fabricated or inaccurate scholarly references. Temporal benchmarks, dynamic verification, knowledge editing, and retrieval-augmented generation offer promising approaches, but none independently guarantees reliable knowledge representation.  The central conclusion is that reliable LLM-generated knowledge requires more than factual recall: it requires temporal awareness, verifiable evidence, accurate source attribution, and explicit recognition of uncertainty. Future systems should therefore integrate continuously updated knowledge sources with robust bibliographic verification and temporal reasoning mechanisms. Such developments are essential for establishing LLMs as dependable tools in academic research and scholarly communication.
