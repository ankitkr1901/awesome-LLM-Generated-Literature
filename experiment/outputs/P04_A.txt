Knowledge Cutoffs, Temporal Knowledge Boundaries, and Citation Reliability in Large Language Models: A Literature Review 1. Introduction Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed information retrieval, academic writing, and knowledge-intensive question answering. However, their ability to generate fluent and authoritative responses does not necessarily imply that their knowledge is current, temporally accurate, or adequately supported by verifiable sources. Three interconnected issues are particularly important: knowledge cutoffs, which delimit the period covered by a model's training data; temporal knowledge boundaries, which concern the validity and evolution of information over time; and citation reliability, which concerns whether references accurately support the claims for which they are provided.  These issues are significant because LLMs frequently present outdated information, combine facts from different historical periods, or generate plausible but nonexistent references. Consequently, evaluating LLM performance requires attention not only to factual correctness but also to the temporal validity, traceability, and evidential quality of generated knowledge.  This review examines the conceptual foundations, principal research approaches, empirical findings, limitations, and outstanding questions concerning these challenges. 2. Knowledge Cutoffs and Temporal Knowledge Boundaries A knowledge cutoff refers to the approximate endpoint of the information incorporated into a model's pretraining or subsequent training data. Unlike conventional databases, LLMs generally do not maintain a continuously updated representation of external reality. Their responses are generated through statistical patterns encoded in model parameters. Therefore, a model trained on information available up to a particular date may lack subsequent developments, including changes in legislation, scientific discoveries, political leadership, institutional policies, and technological standards.  However, a knowledge cutoff should not be interpreted as a perfectly defined temporal boundary. Training datasets may contain documents published after the nominal cutoff, while information acquired earlier may be incompletely represented or inaccurately recalled. Furthermore, fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and external tools can introduce information unavailable during pretraining.  Temporal knowledge boundaries are consequently more complex than a simple distinction between past and present. Facts possess different temporal properties: some are relatively stable, whereas others are time-sensitive. For example, mathematical principles are generally persistent, while stock prices, public officeholders, and clinical recommendations may change substantially. A reliable LLM must therefore distinguish between when information was published, when an event occurred, and the period during which a factual statement remains valid.  This distinction also raises the problem of temporal consistency: a model may correctly reproduce an earlier fact while incorrectly presenting it as current. 3. Temporal Reasoning and the Evaluation of Evolving Knowledge Research increasingly demonstrates that temporal competence is distinct from ordinary factual recall. A model may know that an individual occupied a particular political office but fail to identify the period during which that information was valid.  Tan, Ng, and Bing (2023) address this problem through TempReason, a benchmark designed to assess temporal reasoning across different question types and reasoning levels. Their approach combines temporal-span extraction with time-sensitive reinforcement learning. Experiments involving closed-book and open-book question answering indicate that temporal reasoning can be improved through explicit temporal supervision rather than relying exclusively on general language modelling.  Subsequent research extends this evaluation framework. Chu et al. (2024), through TimeBench, develop a hierarchical benchmark covering diverse temporal reasoning phenomena. Their experiments with models including GPT-4 and LLaMA2 identify substantial differences in performance across temporal reasoning categories and a persistent gap between model and human capabilities. Similarly, Wang and Zhao (2024) introduce TRAM, which integrates ten datasets covering temporal order, duration, frequency, and temporal arithmetic. Their findings indicate that even relatively capable LLMs struggle with structured temporal inference.  A further development is the distinction between temporal reasoning and temporal awareness. Zhu et al. (2025), in EvolveBench, evaluate five dimensions of temporal competence, including cognition, awareness, and trustworthiness. Their framework considers whether models can recognise temporal misalignment between retrieved information and the time specified in a question. This is important because retrieving a factually correct document does not guarantee a temporally appropriate answer.  Collectively, these studies suggest that temporal reliability requires more than remembering dates. Models must associate facts with valid time intervals, interpret temporal expressions, distinguish historical from current information, and recognise when available evidence is insufficient. 4. Knowledge Updating and Retrieval-Augmented Generation Two major approaches to overcoming knowledge limitations are knowledge editing and retrieval-augmented generation.  Knowledge editing attempts to modify information stored within model parameters without retraining the entire model. Yin et al. (2024), in History Matters, introduce the AToKe benchmark for temporal knowledge editing. Their findings reveal an important trade-off: incorporating new information can cause models to forget historical facts. Their proposed Multi-Editing with Time Objective (METO) framework improves the retention of historical information while incorporating updated knowledge.  This research highlights a fundamental distinction between updating a fact and updating a model's temporal understanding. For example, replacing an outdated officeholder's name with a current one is insufficient if the model subsequently misrepresents the historical period during which the former officeholder served.  Retrieval-augmented generation offers an alternative. Lewis et al. (2020) establish a foundational RAG architecture that combines parametric model knowledge with an external, retrievable document collection. Instead of relying exclusively on information acquired during training, the model retrieves relevant passages when answering a query. This approach improves factual specificity and provides opportunities for information provenance.  Vu et al. (2024), in FreshLLMs, investigate the problem of maintaining current knowledge through search-engine augmentation. They introduce FreshQA, a benchmark containing questions about rapidly changing information and false premises. Their evaluation, based on more than 50,000 human judgments, demonstrates that models struggle with dynamic facts and incorrectly framed questions. Their FreshPrompt method improves performance by organising retrieved search information within the model's prompt.  The findings of these studies suggest that retrieval can reduce dependence on static training knowledge, whereas knowledge editing offers more direct control over internal representations. Nevertheless, neither approach completely resolves temporal reliability. Retrieval may produce outdated, contradictory, or irrelevant sources, while editing may damage previously acquired knowledge. Consequently, combining temporal reasoning, reliable retrieval, and explicit historical preservation appears more promising than treating updating as a simple replacement operation. 5. Citation Reliability, Hallucination, and Evidential Attribution Citation reliability constitutes a separate but closely connected challenge. An LLM can generate a plausible bibliographic reference without having verified that the publication exists or supports the associated claim. Three problems must therefore be distinguished: fabricated references, inaccurate bibliographic metadata, and genuine references that do not substantiate the statements attributed to them.  Walters and Wilder (2023) provide empirical evidence through an investigation of ChatGPT-generated literature reviews across 42 multidisciplinary topics. Examining 636 references, they report that 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated. They also identify substantial errors among genuine references. Although these results concern particular models and experimental conditions, they demonstrate that bibliographic fluency should not be confused with reference accuracy.  Gao et al. (2023) address the problem through ALCE (Automatic LLMs' Citation Evaluation), a benchmark assessing answer correctness, fluency, and citation quality. Their findings show that even strong systems frequently fail to provide complete citation support. Importantly, the presence of citations does not automatically establish that individual claims are adequately supported.  Funkquist et al. (2023), through CiteBench, investigate scientific citation-text generation using multiple datasets and evaluation settings. Their work demonstrates the importance of standardised evaluation when assessing how effectively systems synthesise and attribute information from scholarly publications.  Huang et al. (2025) provide a broader conceptual synthesis in their survey of hallucination research. They distinguish different forms and causes of hallucination and examine detection and mitigation approaches. Their analysis reinforces the importance of evaluating factual consistency and evidential faithfulness rather than relying on linguistic plausibility.  These studies collectively establish that citation reliability involves more than bibliographic correctness. A reliable citation must identify an authentic publication, contain accurate metadata, support the associated claim, and provide evidence appropriate to the relevant temporal context. A genuine but outdated reference may therefore be bibliographically valid while being unsuitable evidence for a statement about current conditions. 6. Limitations and Open Research Questions Despite substantial progress, several limitations remain.  First, benchmark coverage is incomplete. Existing temporal reasoning datasets frequently emphasise structured questions and predefined facts. Real-world knowledge, however, involves ambiguous dates, overlapping events, conflicting accounts, and gradual changes. Performance on benchmark tasks may therefore overestimate practical temporal reliability.  Second, knowledge updating remains difficult to evaluate. Knowledge-editing methods can introduce new facts while unintentionally disrupting historical associations. Further research is needed to establish whether models can maintain multiple temporally valid versions of the same fact without interference.  Third, retrieval quality does not guarantee answer quality. Search-based systems depend on the authority, publication date, relevance, and consistency of retrieved documents. Models may select outdated sources, overlook corrections, or combine incompatible information.  Fourth, citation evaluation remains insufficiently granular. Existing approaches assess citation correctness and support, but automated metrics may fail to capture subtle misinterpretations, selective evidence use, or contradictions between sources. Human verification remains important, particularly in legal, medical, and academic contexts.  Several research questions consequently deserve attention: 1. How can LLMs represent the publication date, event date, and validity period of individual facts separately? 2. Can models reliably distinguish historical truth from present-day truth? 3. How can retrieval systems prioritise authoritative, recent, and temporally appropriate evidence? 4. What evaluation frameworks can measure claim-level citation support and bibliographic authenticity simultaneously? 5. Can models communicate uncertainty explicitly when their knowledge is potentially outdated or their sources conflict? Future research should prioritise integrated evaluation frameworks combining temporal reasoning, source provenance, factual verification, and citation accuracy. 7. Conclusion and Synthesis The literature demonstrates that knowledge cutoffs, temporal reasoning, and citation reliability are interconnected dimensions of LLM trustworthiness. Static parametric knowledge creates vulnerabilities when information changes, while weak temporal reasoning makes it difficult to distinguish historical facts from current realities. Retrieval and knowledge editing provide promising solutions, but introduce challenges involving source quality, temporal consistency, and historical knowledge preservation.  Similarly, citation generation does not necessarily establish evidential reliability. Authentic references, accurate metadata, claim-level support, and temporal appropriateness must all be evaluated independently.  Overall, the transition towards trustworthy LLMs requires moving beyond the measurement of factual recall towards temporally grounded, evidence-supported, and independently verifiable knowledge generation. Future systems should not merely provide answers; they should also communicate when information was valid, where it originated, and how confidently it can be supported.
